The 2026 result

After the 2026 election, Parliament would have 131 seats, 11 more than usual

Under MMP, you have 2 votes. Your party vote decides how many seats each party gets in Parliament. Your electorate vote chooses your local MP.

Electorate MPs normally count as part of their party’s share of seats. In 2026, 2 parties won more electorates than their party vote gives them.

These figures use the preliminary count from election night. Official results are due on 27 November.

Overhang seats

National won 42 electorates, but its party vote covers only 35 of them

Te Pāti Māori won 5 electorates, but its party vote covers only 1.

A party keeps every electorate it wins, so Parliament grows to fit the seats that aren’t covered. That’s 11 overhang seats, the most since MMP began in 1996.

Split votes · National

Many people who voted for a National candidate gave their party vote to ACT or NZ First

We estimate about 272,000 people in National’s 42 electorates gave their electorate vote to the National candidate and their party vote to another party. About 155,000 of them party-voted ACT, and about 63,000 party-voted NZ First.

Their party votes counted in full for ACT and NZ First. Their electorate votes helped elect National’s 7 overhang MPs.

Split votes · Te Pāti Māori

Most of Te Pāti Māori’s electorate votes came from Green and Labour voters

Te Pāti Māori got 13,845 party votes across the country. Its 5 winning candidates got about 68,000 electorate votes.

We estimate about 61,000 of those came from people who gave their party vote to another party, mostly the Green Party (about 30,000) and Labour (about 25,000).

The overall result

The two overhangs mostly cancel out, but Parliament grows by 11

National, ACT and NZ First won 50.4% of the party vote for parties in Parliament. That’s about 66.0 of 131 seats. They got 67, a majority by 1 seat.

Labour, the Green Party and Te Pāti Māori won 43.6%, about 57.2 seats. They got 57. The Opportunity Party won 6.0%, about 7.9 seats. It got 7.

These figures leave out votes for parties that didn’t get into Parliament.

He huarahi hou · A different approach

What if the rules were different?

So far, this is what happened under the current rules. The rest of this page looks at a proposed change to the law, and how the 2026 result would look under it.

How it would work · National

7 of National’s seats aren’t covered by its party vote

Think of each electorate seat as needing to be covered by party votes. National’s party vote covers 35 of the 42 electorates it won. The other 7 are empty.

The change only applies when a party has empty seats like these. It fills them with the party votes of people who split their vote: they gave a National candidate their electorate vote, but gave their party vote to someone else.

How it would work · National

48% of each split vote would count for National

It starts with about 2,800 party votes for parties that didn’t get into Parliament. Then the same share of every other split vote moves, until National’s party vote covers its 42 electorates. That share is 48%.

That’s about 132,000 votes. Most come from ACT (about 74,000) and NZ First (about 30,000) voters, because they made up most of the split votes.

How it would work · Te Pāti Māori

Te Pāti Māori’s party vote covers 1 of its 5 seats

The same applies to Te Pāti Māori. It won 5 electorates, and its party vote covers 1 of them, so 4 seats are empty.

How it would work · Te Pāti Māori

Every split vote for a Te Pāti Māori candidate would count for the party

Most people who gave a Te Pāti Māori candidate their electorate vote gave their party vote to the Green Party or Labour. Under the change, all of those votes, about 60,000, would count for Te Pāti Māori. That covers 4 of its 5 electorates.

The fifth seat comes off the other parties’ list seats, as the Independent Electoral Review recommended, so Parliament still has 120 seats.

The result

Every electorate keeps its MP, and Parliament has 120 seats

National keeps its 42 seats and Te Pāti Māori keeps 5. ACT has 4 fewer seats and NZ First 2 fewer, because many of their voters gave their electorate vote to National. Labour has 3 fewer and the Green Party 2 fewer, mostly because many of their voters gave their electorate vote to Te Pāti Māori.

National, ACT and NZ First would still have a majority, with 61 of 120 seats.

Proportional representation

Each side’s seats are closer to its party vote

With the change, National, ACT and NZ First get 61 of 120 seats. Their party vote gives them about 60.4. Labour, the Green Party and Te Pāti Māori get 52, against about 52.4. The Opportunity Party gets 7, against about 7.2.

The Opportunity Party isn’t counted in either group.

  • ACT, NZ First, Labour and the Green Party keep all their list seats

With the proposed change

  • 48% of National’s split votes, and all of Te Pāti Māori’s, count for the electorate vote’s party
  • ACT 9 (4 fewer), NZ First 10 (2 fewer), Labour 33 (3 fewer), Green Party 14 (2 fewer)

Ngā pātai · Common questions

Does this change my vote?

Would my party vote be thrown away?

No. If part of your party vote moves, it counts for the party of the candidate you gave your electorate vote to. You chose both.

Why would ACT and NZ First lose seats?

Many of their voters gave their electorate vote to a National candidate. That helped National win more electorates than its party vote covers. Under the change, part of those party votes would count for National instead.

Does it only affect one side?

No. In 2026 it affects both. Votes move to National from ACT and NZ First voters, and to Te Pāti Māori from Labour and Green voters.

Could a small party drop out of Parliament?

No. The 5% threshold is checked before any votes move.

Why not just leave out the extra MPs?

Germany does this. In 2026, it would leave 7 National electorates and 4 Māori electorates without the MP their voters chose.

Are these the final numbers?

No. They use the preliminary count. Special votes and the official split-vote figures could change the result by a seat or so.

This describes a proposal for discussion. It is not current electoral law.

How we worked this out. We used the preliminary count from election night (7 November 2026) from the Electoral Commission as if it were final. Special votes aren’t included. Official results are due on 27 November.

The Electoral Commission doesn’t publish split-vote figures until the official results, so we estimated them. For each electorate National or Te Pāti Māori won, we assumed each party’s voters split their votes the same way as in 2023 (using general electorates for National and Māori electorates for Te Pāti Māori). We then scaled the numbers so they add up to the winning candidate’s 2026 vote. Electorate boundaries changed in 2025, so this is an approximation.

If moving all of a party’s split votes isn’t enough to cover its overhang, the rest comes off other parties’ list seats, as the Independent Electoral Review recommended. In 2026 this applies to 1 Te Pāti Māori seat.

National, ACT and NZ First are grouped together, as are Labour, the Green Party and Te Pāti Māori. The Opportunity Party isn’t counted in either group.

We tested other assumptions about how people split their votes. National, ACT and NZ First get 61 or 62 seats in every case, ACT 9 or 10, NZ First 10 or 11, and the Green Party 14 or 15.

Seats are allocated with the Sainte-Laguë formula used in New Zealand elections. In the split-vote pictures, 1 dot is about 5,000 votes.
