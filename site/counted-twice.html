One ballot, counted twice

Under MMP, your party vote decides how many seats each party gets in Parliament. In 2023, split voting in 6 Māori electorates meant some parties got more seats than their share of the party vote.

Find out what happened, and how a proposed change to the rules would fix it.

The 2023 result

In 2023, Parliament ended up with 122 seats, 2 more than usual

Under MMP, you have 2 votes. Your party vote decides how many seats each party gets in Parliament. Your electorate vote chooses your local MP.

Electorate MPs normally count as part of their party’s share of seats. That way, the party vote decides the overall result. In 2023, it didn’t quite work that way.

A by-election in Port Waikato later added a 123rd seat. It isn’t included here.

Overhang seats

Te Pāti Māori won 6 electorates, but its party vote covered only 4 of them

When a party wins more electorates than its party vote covers, it keeps them all. The seats that aren’t covered are called overhang seats. Parliament grows to fit them, and every other party still gets its full share.

Split votes

Many of those electorate votes came from people who party-voted Labour or Green

Across the 6 electorates Te Pāti Māori won, about 41,000 people gave their electorate vote to its candidate and their party vote to another party. In Waiariki alone, 59% of people who party-voted Labour gave their electorate vote to Rawiri Waititi.

These voters helped elect the 2 overhang MPs, and their party votes still counted in full for Labour and the Greens.

The overall result

Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori got more seats than their party vote gives them

Together they won 44.1% of the party vote for parties in Parliament. That’s about 53.8 of the 122 seats. They got 55.

National, ACT and NZ First won 55.9%, which is about 68.2 seats. They got 67.

These figures leave out votes for parties that didn’t get into Parliament.

He huarahi hou · A different approach

What if the rules were different?

So far, this is what happened under the current rules. The rest of this page looks at a proposed change to the law, and how the 2023 result would have looked under it.

How it would work

2 of Te Pāti Māori’s seats aren’t covered by its party vote

Think of each electorate seat as needing to be covered by party votes. Te Pāti Māori’s party vote covers 4 of the 6 electorates it won. The other 2 are empty.

The change only applies when a party has empty seats like these. It fills them with the party votes of people who split their vote: they gave a Te Pāti Māori candidate their electorate vote, but gave their party vote to someone else.

How it would work · 1

First, votes for parties that didn’t get into Parliament

About 3,000 of them gave their party vote to a party that didn’t get into Parliament. Under the current rules, those votes don’t elect anyone. Under the proposal, they’d count for Te Pāti Māori instead.

That’s not enough to cover even 1 seat.

How it would work · 2

Then, the same share of every other split vote

Te Pāti Māori’s party vote needs about 35,000 more votes to cover its 6 electorate seats. So the same share of each remaining split vote counts for Te Pāti Māori instead. In 2023, that share was:

84%

These aren’t new votes. Every one comes from someone who gave Te Pāti Māori their electorate vote. Most come from Labour and Green voters, because they made up most of the split votes.

The result

Te Pāti Māori keeps its 6 MPs, and Parliament stays at 120 seats

Every electorate keeps the MP its voters chose. The 2 seats come from Labour and the Green Party, 1 each, because it was mostly their voters who split. National, ACT and NZ First don’t change.

In 2023, the same parties would have formed the Government. In a closer election, 1 or 2 seats could decide it.

Proportional representation

Seats match the party vote again

With the change, Labour, the Green Party and Te Pāti Māori would get 53 of 120 seats. Their party vote gives them about 52.9.

National, ACT and NZ First would get 67, against about 67.1.

The rule would work the same way for any party with overhang seats, on either side of politics.

Current rules

122 seats

  • Te Pāti Māori keeps its 6 electorate MPs
  • Labour and the Green Party keep all their list seats
  • Parliament gets 2 extra seats
  • Labour, Green and Te Pāti Māori get 45.1% of the seats with 44.1% of the party vote

With the proposed change

120 seats

  • Te Pāti Māori keeps its 6 electorate MPs
  • 84% of split votes count for the electorate vote’s party
  • Labour 33 (1 fewer), Green Party 14 (1 fewer)
  • Labour, Green and Te Pāti Māori get 44.2% of the seats with 44.1% of the party vote

Ngā pātai · Common questions

Does this change my vote?

Would my party vote be thrown away?

No. If part of your party vote moves, it counts for the party of the candidate you gave your electorate vote to. You chose both.

Would it change who wins my electorate?

No. Every electorate winner keeps their seat. Only list seats change.

Does it only affect parties on the left?

No. It applies to any party with overhang seats. If National won more electorate seats than its party vote gave it, some party votes would move to National instead.

Could a small party drop out of Parliament?

No. The 5% threshold is checked before any votes move.

Why not just leave out the extra MPs?

Germany does this. Here, it would have left 2 Māori electorates, Te Tai Tokerau and Tāmaki Makaurau, without the MP their voters chose.

Would this happen every election?

Probably not. If splitting your vote can’t win extra seats, there’s less reason to do it tactically.

This describes a proposal for discussion. It is not current electoral law.
